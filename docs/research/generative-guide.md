# Generative interview guide

**Run this before refining any screen.** Masterplan section 13 names the sequencing risk plainly: the priority artifacts were built before generative research, which was the right call for a prototype whose job is to be testable, and the wrong call to carry forward.

**Who:** five to eight couples currently planning, four to fourteen months out. Both partners. 45 minutes separately, then 20 minutes together.

**What this answers:** whether a calm, execution-first agent is the felt need, or whether that is the design team's taste wearing a user's clothes.

---

## What would mean we were wrong

Write these down before the first session and check them honestly afterward. If none of them can happen in the session as scripted, the script is broken.

| The thesis | What would falsify it |
|---|---|
| Wedding planning feels overwhelming, and lowering the temperature is the value | Couples describe planning as genuinely fun and want *more* surface area, more options, more browsing. Relief is not what they are shopping for. |
| Couples want work taken off their plate | They describe the choosing itself as the point, and delegation as loss. They want a better catalogue, not an agent. |
| Decisions are shared and the product should hold them open | One partner is happily and explicitly the decider, and forcing joint approval would be friction, not fairness. |
| Trust needs to be visible, with sources and dates | Nobody asks where a number came from, and evidence reads as clutter rather than reassurance. |
| The unit of value is one clear next action | They are working several threads at once by choice and a single foreground decision feels patronising. |

Two or more of these landing means the product thesis needs revisiting, not the interface.

---

## Separate sessions, one partner at a time

Run these apart. The point is to hear each person's version before the household's official version forms in the room.

### Opening, 5 minutes

> "I'm not going to show you anything for a while. I mostly want to hear how your planning is actually going. There are no right answers, and I didn't design what we might look at later, so you can't hurt my feelings."

The last clause is a small lie in service of honesty. Use it.

### The last two weeks, 15 minutes

Anchor everything in what already happened. Never ask what they would do.

> "Walk me through the last two weeks of planning. Start wherever it started."

Follow the thread. When something happened, get concrete:

- "Where were you when you did that?"
- "How long did that take, start to finish?"
- "Who else was involved?"
- "What did you have to look up or wait for?"
- "What happened right before you gave up on it?"

**Listen for, do not ask about:** where the work is happening (phone at night, spreadsheet at work, group chat), how many places information lives, what has stalled and for how long, what they have already stopped doing.

### The division of it, 10 minutes

> "How did it end up that you're the one doing that part?"

> "What's the last thing your partner decided on their own?"

> "Is there anything you'd rather they not see?"

That third question is uncomfortable and it is the most important one in the guide. The product claims private preferences alongside shared decisions. If nobody wants privacy, that feature is invention. If they do, this is where you learn what kind.

### Delegation, 10 minutes

> "Who else is doing wedding work for you right now?"

If they have a planner, a relative, or a friend helping:

- "What did you tell them they could just handle?"
- "What did they do that you wished they'd asked about first?"
- "What did they check with you on that you wished they'd just done?"

**This is the highest-value section in the guide.** The delegation model is the product's riskiest framework and it is currently pure assumption. These three questions are the closest thing to evidence available before launch, because they describe real delegation the participant has already lived through.

### The worst of it, 5 minutes

> "What's the part of this you've been avoiding?"

> "Has anything about it caused an argument? You don't have to tell me what."

---

## Together, 20 minutes

Bring both partners in. Say nothing about what either said separately.

> "Tell me about the last thing you disagreed on."

Then let them tell it. Watch how they narrate it more than what they say: who talks first, who defers, whether they laugh, whether one summarises for the other and the other corrects them.

> "How did it get resolved?"

If it did not resolve:

> "What's it waiting on?"

**Watch for:** whether "we decided" means both of them, whether one partner learns something in this conversation they did not know, whether either performs agreement they had not shown separately.

### Only at the very end

If, and only if, the calm-versus-catalogue question is still genuinely open after everything above:

> "If something could take one part of this off your plate entirely, without you having to check it, which part?"

Then stop. Do not describe VowOS. Do not show a screen. The moment you describe the product, you have converted a generative session into a pitch, and everything after it is politeness.

---

## Writing it up

Same day, while you still remember tone. One page:

1. What they are actually doing, in their words, not paraphrased into ours.
2. Every instance of real delegation, and how it went.
3. Which falsification rows above got closer to true.
4. The quote you cannot stop thinking about.

The fourth item is not decoration. In a small sample the thing that snags is usually the finding, and it is the first thing lost to a tidy summary.
